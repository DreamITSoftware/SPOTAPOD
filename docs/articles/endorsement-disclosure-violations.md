# What Would It Take to Prove Endorsement-Disclosure Violations?

### "Partnered with," "sponsored," "affiliate link": the scan checks for the words, not for whether anyone disclosed

The FTC's Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255) require a visible disclosure whenever there's a material connection between an endorser and a brand: a sponsorship, a partnership, an affiliate relationship. Checking LinkedIn content for language suggesting that kind of relationship, without a visible disclosure nearby, turns up a measurable pattern. The pattern can prove only so much.

## The numbers

Checked across three datasets for an "endorsement disclosure gap," phrases like "partnered with," "sponsored," or "affiliate link" appearing without an accompanying visible disclosure marker, the rates run **1.27%** in one dataset, up to **3.02%** in another. That is a share of content carrying language associated with a material commercial relationship.

## What this method cannot do

A record counts as a "hit" here if any qualifying phrase appears anywhere in its text, case-insensitively, with no context check at all. There's no sentiment analysis, no verification that a disclosure doesn't exist somewhere else in the post that the pattern simply didn't catch, no distinction between literal and figurative use. "This partnership changed my career" and an actual undisclosed paid promotion trip the same pattern.

That is the limit of what a keyword count can establish. Proving an actual Endorsement Guides violation requires the material connection to be real, the disclosure to be genuinely absent rather than merely missing from where an automated check looked, and the endorsement to be genuinely about goods or services rather than a figurative use of "partnership" language. None of that is something an aggregate text scan can verify.

## Why report the pattern

A base rate this consistent, across three independently sourced datasets, shows that language associated with sponsorship arrangements is common in this content. It is a place to look, not a conclusion. Nothing here identifies a specific record, author, or brand relationship, the same as every other legal-adjacent finding in this project.

## What this is, and isn't

**VERIFIED**, reproducible: the keyword-match rates above.

**INFERENCE**: that any specific match represents an actual, undisclosed material connection. No comparison baseline was scanned to establish whether these rates are unusual for social content generally.
