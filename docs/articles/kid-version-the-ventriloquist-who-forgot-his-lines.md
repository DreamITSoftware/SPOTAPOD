# The Ventriloquist Who Forgot His Lines

### A true story about a trick that got caught, 9,780 times

Have you ever seen a ventriloquist? That's a performer who sits a wooden puppet on their knee and makes it look like the puppet is talking. The puppet's mouth moves. A funny voice comes out. And for a second, your brain almost believes a toy is really speaking, even though you know the person holding it is doing all the talking without moving their own lips.

The whole trick only works if nobody notices the puppet is just repeating lines someone else wrote. If the performer ever read their secret notes out loud by mistake, instead of the real joke, everyone in the audience would know right away: oh, this was never the puppet talking at all.

That exact mistake happened, for real, 9,780 times, on a website called LinkedIn, where grown-ups post about their jobs.

## The mistake that gave it away

Imagine a comment posted under somebody's photo that says: "What a nice post {{firstName}}!"

See that part in the curly brackets, `{{firstName}}`? That's not a name. That's a sticky note that was supposed to get swapped out for a real name before anyone saw it, like a placeholder in a fill-in-the-blank worksheet that nobody filled in. A computer program was supposed to grab the real person's name and slot it in. Instead, the placeholder itself got posted, blank spot and all, for the whole world to see.

This wasn't just one oops. Out of 77,899 comments checked in one big pile of data, 9,780 of them, that's about 1 out of every 8, still had their fill-in-the-blank sticky notes showing. One out of eight isn't an accident. That's a trick that keeps breaking, over and over, in front of everybody.

## The silliest broken comment of all

Some of the broken comments were even funnier. A trick called "word swapping" lets a computer pick a different word each time, so it doesn't say the exact same thing over and over, which would look suspicious. It's like giving the puppet a menu of jokes to choose from: "Great," or "Awesome," or "Golden." Normally the puppet just picks one and says it.

But sometimes the menu got posted by accident, curly brackets, pipe marks and all, like this: `{{Great|Awesome|Unreal|Nice|Golden}}`. That's the whole secret menu, right there in public, instead of just one word picked from it.

And the silliest one of all: `{{|||||||||}}`. That's a menu with nothing written on it at all. Just empty lines. The computer didn't even have a joke ready, and it posted the empty menu anyway, like a waiter handing you a blank piece of paper and calling it the specials.

## Who was holding the puppet?

Here's the part that matters most. All 9,780 of these broken comments only came from 144 different accounts. Not thousands of random people, just 144. And guess what most of those 144 accounts say they do for a job? A whole lot of them call themselves career coaches, the kind of grown-up who gets paid to teach other people how to be successful at work.

One single account, a "Leadership Coach," had its secret sticky note show up 1,008 times. Another one, which bragged about helping raise over a billion dollars for companies, did it 882 times. These weren't random accidents happening to innocent bystanders. These were the same kind of accounts this whole investigation had already caught faking their popularity before, caught again, this time with their hand visibly still on the puppet.

## Why this one is easy to believe

Most of the tricky things found in this investigation needed some math to prove, counting percentages, running special tests to make sure a pattern wasn't just random luck. This one doesn't need any of that. You don't need to be a math whiz to know that a real person doesn't type curly brackets and the word "firstName" by accident, especially not over and over, especially not from accounts already caught faking things before. You can just read it and see it for yourself. That's rare, and it's worth saying plainly: this is the clearest, most obvious proof in the whole story.

## What this doesn't prove

It's important to be fair here too. This doesn't prove every single comment from those 144 accounts was fake, only the ones where the mistake actually showed. A puppet that gets its lines right most nights and messes up one night out of eight is still a puppet every single night, we just can't always see the strings. One out of eight broken comments is the smallest possible guess at how much was fake, not the biggest.

It also doesn't prove the people running these accounts knew their tool was broken. Maybe they paid for a service to make their posts look more popular and never checked closely on how it actually worked. That's still not honest, but it's a different kind of not-honest than doing it on purpose and knowing exactly how broken it was.

## The honest wrap-up

**We know for sure:** 9,780 real comments, out of 77,899 checked, still had their secret fill-in-the-blank notes showing instead of real words. Those all traced back to just 144 accounts, and the biggest ones among them were career coaches, the same kind of accounts already caught faking popularity earlier in this investigation.

**We don't know for sure:** how many of the other comments from those same accounts were also fake but didn't get caught, and whether the people running the accounts knew their tool was broken or just never checked.

**A reasonable guess, not a proven fact:** that this broken trick is connected to the bigger pattern of fake popularity already found in this investigation, since it's the exact same accounts doing it.

Every number in this story can be checked by anyone, using the same public files this investigation used. That's the whole point: don't just trust a confident-sounding claim, check it yourself whenever you can.
