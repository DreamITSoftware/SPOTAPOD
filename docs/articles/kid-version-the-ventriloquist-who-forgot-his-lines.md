# The Ventriloquist Who Forgot His Lines

### A true story about a trick that got caught, 9,780 times

A ventriloquist is a performer who sits a wooden puppet on their knee and makes it look like the puppet is talking. The puppet's mouth moves and a funny voice comes out, while the performer does all the talking without moving their own lips.

The trick only works if nobody notices the puppet is repeating lines someone else wrote. If the performer read their secret notes out loud by mistake, instead of the joke, the audience would know right away that the puppet was never talking.

That mistake happened 9,780 times on a website called LinkedIn, where grown-ups post about their jobs.

## The mistake that gave it away

Here is a comment posted under somebody's photo: "What a nice post {{firstName}}!"

The part in curly brackets, `{{firstName}}`, is a placeholder. A computer program was supposed to swap it for the real person's name before anyone saw it, like a fill-in-the-blank worksheet. Instead, the placeholder itself got posted.

This happened many times. Out of 77,899 comments checked in one set of data, 9,780 of them, about 1 out of every 8, still showed the placeholder. That many cannot be a one-off mistake.

## The strangest broken comments

Some broken comments were stranger. "Word swapping" lets a computer pick a different word each time, so it doesn't say the same thing over and over, which would look suspicious. It works like a menu of words the puppet can choose from: "Great," or "Awesome," or "Golden." Normally the puppet picks one and says it.

Sometimes the whole menu got posted by accident, curly brackets and pipe marks included, like this: `{{Great|Awesome|Unreal|Nice|Golden}}`. That is the whole menu, posted in public instead of one word picked from it.

Another was `{{|||||||||}}`, a menu with nothing written on it. The computer had no word to pick and posted the empty menu anyway.

## Who was holding the puppet?

All 9,780 of these broken comments came from only 144 different accounts. Many of those accounts call themselves career coaches, grown-ups who are paid to teach other people how to be successful at work.

One account, a "Leadership Coach," showed the placeholder 1,008 times. Another, which claimed to have helped raise over a billion dollars for companies, did it 882 times. These were the same kind of accounts this investigation had already found faking their popularity.

## Why this one is easy to believe

Most findings in this investigation needed math to prove, such as percentages and tests to make sure a pattern wasn't random luck. This one does not. A real person does not type curly brackets and the word "firstName" by accident, over and over. You can read it and see it yourself. This is the clearest proof in the whole story.

## What this doesn't prove

This does not prove every comment from those 144 accounts was fake, only the ones where the mistake showed. The 1 in 8 figure counts only the comments where the mistake was visible, so it is the smallest estimate of how much was fake, not the largest.

It also does not prove the people running these accounts knew their tool was broken. They may have paid for a service to make their posts look more popular and never checked how it worked. That is still dishonest, but it is different from doing it on purpose with full knowledge of the problem.

## Is there a law about this?

In the United States, a government group called the Federal Trade Commission, or FTC, protects people from being tricked, especially when money or trust is involved.

The FTC has a rule, officially 16 CFR Section 465.8, that says companies and people may not use fake "indicators of social media influence" to look more popular, trusted, or important than they are. "Indicators of social media influence" means likes, comments, followers, and the other numbers that show how many people like something.

Suppose a stranger says their lemonade stand is the best in town, and a long line of customers seems to prove it. If the stranger secretly paid ten friends to stand in line and look excited without tasting the lemonade, the line still looks convincing but proves nothing. The FTC rule exists because the same trick, done with likes and comments instead of a pretend line, can fool just as many people, and on a screen nobody can see who is behind it.

This investigation is careful here. Finding broken placeholders like `{{firstName}}` is strong evidence that something automated was happening. But "something automated happened" is different from "this specific person broke this specific law on purpose." Proving that usually requires showing they knew what they were doing and meant to trick people, which this investigation's data cannot show by itself. So nobody in this story is called a criminal. What can be said is that the behavior documented here, accounts faking engagement through broken automated tools, is the kind of thing the rule was written to stop, whether or not any one account is ever formally punished.

A law can describe something as wrong, and evidence can show it happening, without every instance ending in a court case. Laws work partly by stopping bad behavior directly and partly by making the rules known, so companies like LinkedIn have a reason to keep looking for cheaters before any judge is involved.

## Summary

**We know for sure:** 9,780 real comments, out of 77,899 checked, still had their secret fill-in-the-blank notes showing instead of real words. Those all traced back to 144 accounts, and the biggest ones were career coaches, the same kind of accounts found faking popularity earlier in this investigation.

**We don't know for sure:** how many of the other comments from those same accounts were also fake but didn't get caught, and whether the people running the accounts knew their tool was broken or just never checked.

**A reasonable guess, not a proven fact:** that this broken trick is connected to the larger pattern of fake popularity found in this investigation, since the same accounts are involved. It is also only a guess that any specific account broke the FTC's rule on purpose, since proving "on purpose" takes more than this data can show.

Every number in this story can be checked by anyone, using the same public files this investigation used. Don't just trust a confident-sounding claim. Check it yourself when you can.
