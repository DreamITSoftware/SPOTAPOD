# State Deceptive-Practices Law and Engagement Pods

### The person actually deceived by a fake like count usually isn't the pod service's own customer

Federal law isn't the only angle here, and in one specific way, state law reaches something federal law structurally can't: a reader who never bought anything from anyone.

## New York's law, and the gap in its third element

New York General Business Law § 349 bars "deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any business." It requires consumer-oriented conduct with broad public impact, a materially misleading act judged by whether it's likely to mislead a reasonable consumer, and actual injury caused by the deception. Unlike 16 CFR § 465.8, it needs no scienter, and it carries a private right of action with a statutory minimum per violation.

That third element, actual injury to a specific person, is where this gets interesting for engagement pods specifically. The person most directly deceived by an inflated like count isn't the pod service's paying customer, who knows exactly what they bought. It's the third-party reader who encounters a post with hundreds of likes and takes that as a signal the content is worth trusting. This project treats that mechanism as an inference, not a measured outcome, precisely because no dataset can show who actually read a given post or what they decided because of it. Without a specific misled reader who can show actual injury, § 349's third element has no factual home in aggregate data alone.

## California's borrowing statute

California's Unfair Competition Law (Business & Professions Code § 17200) defines unfair competition as any "unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice." Its "unlawful" prong is a borrowing statute. Once any other law's violation is established, a § 465.8 violation, an FTC Act § 5 violation, a § 349 violation, it becomes independently actionable under § 17200 too, with its own remedies. It adds no new element to check against data on its own. It just widens who can sue once someone else's violation is separately proven.

## Why state law matters here at all

Federal frameworks like § 465.8 are built around a seller-buyer transaction. State deceptive-practices law is built around a misled member of the public, which is closer to what actually happens when a reader trusts an inflated engagement number. That's a real structural difference, not a technicality, even though neither this project nor any dataset in it can supply the one missing fact both frameworks eventually need: an actual, identifiable person who was actually misled.

## What this is, and isn't

**Legal framework, not a finding**: this project does not claim any specific violation of either statute has occurred.
