# The FTC's Own Playbook: Comparing Your Ceiling Figure to Real Settlements

### $18 billion in theoretical exposure. $43,086 in an actual judgment. That gap is the whole story.

Every enforcement regime has a gap between its statutory ceiling and what actually gets collected. Engagement pods are no exception, and the size of the gap here is worth sitting with.

## The two numbers, side by side

Multiply three LinkedIn engagement-pod datasets' combined record count by the FTC's current maximum civil penalty per violation and you get **$18,093,929,952**, a deliberately extreme arithmetic exercise, built on the unsupported assumption that every single exported record counts as its own maximum-penalty violation.

The real-world comparison: **LinkedIn Corp. v. TopSocial24 et al.** resulted in a **$43,086** judgment against a comparable engagement-pod-style service in 2023.

Eighteen billion versus forty-three thousand. The theoretical ceiling is roughly 420,000 times the real settlement.

## Why the gap is this large, and what it actually tells you

A theoretical ceiling assumes maximum penalty, maximum violation count, and maximum enforcement intent, all at once, conditions that essentially never hold in a real case. Real settlements reflect what a court or negotiating parties actually decided a specific set of facts was worth, discounted for litigation risk, proof difficulty, and every element a rule like 16 CFR § 465.8 requires that a dataset alone can't establish (scienter, chief among them).

The lesson isn't "the ceiling is fake, ignore it." It's that a theoretical maximum and a realistic outcome are measuring two completely different things, and treating the first as a stand-in for the second, in either direction, to make a case look scarier or a risk look smaller, is a category error.

## What this means for anyone citing a big number in this space

If you're going to cite a dollar figure about engagement-pod liability, cite the one with a defendant, a docket number, and an actual judgment attached, not the one that comes from multiplying a record count by a statutory maximum. One of those numbers describes something that happened. The other describes an assumption nobody has ever actually charged a case under.
