# The Day I Deleted My Own Draft

### An article was fully written, and then it got deleted, because one of its central claims couldn't be independently verified

Not every piece of writing that gets produced for this project gets published. This is the story of one that didn't, and why deleting a finished draft was the correct outcome rather than a failure.

## What happened

Early in this project, a commentary piece was drafted contrasting this project's own findings with a specific third-party document's disputed statistics. The draft was complete: argument built, numbers cited, structure done. Before publishing, the standing rule applied. Every disputed or borrowed statistic gets checked directly against raw data before it's allowed to appear as anything stronger than STATED or UNCORROBORATED.

Some of the numbers checked out. Some didn't survive contact with the raw data at all, not "off by a little," but unverifiable in the form they'd been asserted. Rather than soften the claims into vaguer language and publish anyway, the draft was deleted outright and rebuilt from scratch, using only numbers that had actually been independently recomputed.

## Why deletion, not editing

Editing a flawed draft down to only its verified claims sounds like the more efficient move, and in some cases it is. This time it wasn't, because the unverified claims weren't isolated sentences. They were load-bearing parts of the argument's structure. Removing them wouldn't have left a smaller, honest version of the same piece. It would have left an incoherent one. The honest response to a draft built on a claim that doesn't hold up isn't a patch. It's starting over.

## The instruction this follows

This project operates under an explicit standing rule: when something would require asserting a claim as fact that can't actually be verified, stop and flag it rather than finding a workaround. A workaround here would have looked like softening language just enough to keep the draft technically defensible while preserving its original, more dramatic framing. That's exactly the move the rule exists to prevent.

## Why this story is worth telling at all

A visible instance of "we wrote something, checked it, and threw it away" is more convincing evidence of a project's actual standards than any amount of stated commitment to rigor. Anyone can claim to fact-check. Fewer projects can point to a specific piece of finished work they deleted because it didn't hold up.

## What this is, and isn't

**STATED, by this project about its own process**: this deletion happened, for the reasons described.

**Not named**: which specific claims failed verification in that draft. The corrected version of the underlying commentary, with accurate numbers, is published separately in this project's repository.
