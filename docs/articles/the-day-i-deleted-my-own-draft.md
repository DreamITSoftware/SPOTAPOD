# The Day I Deleted My Own Draft

### An article was fully written, and then it got deleted, because one of its central claims couldn't be independently verified

Not every piece of writing that gets produced for this project gets published. This is about one that didn't, and why deleting a finished draft was the correct outcome.

## What happened

Early in this project, a commentary piece was drafted contrasting this project's own findings with a specific third-party document's disputed statistics. The draft was complete: argument built, numbers cited, structure done. Before publishing, the standing rule applied. Every disputed or borrowed statistic gets checked directly against raw data before it's allowed to appear as anything stronger than STATED or UNCORROBORATED.

Some of the numbers checked out. Some failed against the raw data entirely. They were not off by a little. They were unverifiable in the form they'd been asserted. Rather than soften the claims into vaguer language and publish anyway, the draft was deleted outright and rebuilt from scratch, using only numbers that had actually been independently recomputed.

## Why deletion instead of editing

Editing a flawed draft down to its verified claims is often more efficient. This time it wasn't, because the unverified claims were not isolated sentences. The argument depended on them. Removing them would have left an incoherent piece, not a smaller honest one. A draft built on a claim that doesn't hold up needs to be started over.

## The instruction this follows

This project operates under an explicit standing rule: when something would require asserting a claim as fact that can't actually be verified, stop and flag it rather than finding a workaround. A workaround here would have meant softening the language enough to keep the draft technically defensible while preserving its original, more dramatic framing. The rule exists to prevent that.

## Why I'm telling this

A specific case of "we wrote something, checked it, and threw it away" shows a project's standards better than a stated commitment to rigor. Anyone can claim to fact-check. Fewer projects can point to a finished piece they deleted because it didn't hold up.

## What is stated and what is not named

**Not named**: which specific claims failed verification in that draft. The corrected version of the underlying commentary, with accurate numbers, is published separately in this project's repository.
