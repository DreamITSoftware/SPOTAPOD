# A Letter to a Job Seeker Who Just Got Rejected

### A different format: direct address in the second person, based on this project's rejection-content research

Most of this series speaks about the data. This one speaks to someone specific: a person who just got a rejection email and is scrolling LinkedIn afterward, encountering a wall of other people's curated success.

## What you're looking at

The career-success and "here's how I did it" content in your feed is not a representative sample of what is happening to people in your position. This project's own research into this platform's content found heavy duplication in the categories most likely to make a rejection sting more: identical resume-rewrite templates (533 of them, independently verified) circulating as if they were 533 independent success stories. It also found that in some datasets a small number of highly active accounts produce a disproportionate share of all visible content. What looks like a consensus of other people succeeding easily is, in measurable part, the same small set of voices and the same templates, repeated.

## What that doesn't mean

It does not mean nobody is succeeding, and it does not mean your rejection wasn't real or doesn't matter. It means the feeling that "everyone else has this figured out except me," produced by scrolling a feed built in part around engagement mechanics this project has documented, is not a reliable read of reality. The sample you see is distorted by how the feed works, not by your own shortcomings.

## The practical takeaway

If a viral post's advice made you feel worse rather than more equipped, trust that reaction over the post's engagement count. This project's research suggests engagement counts say much less about a post's value than they appear to.
