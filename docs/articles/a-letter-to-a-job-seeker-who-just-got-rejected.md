# A Letter to a Job Seeker Who Just Got Rejected

### A different format, direct address, second person, grounded in this project's rejection-content research rather than in a chart or a percentage

Most of this series speaks about the data. This one speaks to someone specific: a person who just got a rejection email and is scrolling LinkedIn afterward, encountering a wall of other people's curated success.

## What you're actually looking at

The career-success and "here's how I did it" content filling your feed right now isn't a representative sample of what's actually happening to people in your position. This project's own research into this platform's content found heavy duplication in exactly the categories most likely to make a rejection sting more, identical resume-rewrite templates (533 of them, independently verified) circulating as if they were 533 independent success stories, and account-concentration patterns showing a small number of highly active accounts producing a disproportionate share of all visible content in some datasets. What looks like an overwhelming consensus of other people succeeding easily is, in real and measurable part, the same small set of voices and the same templates, repeated.

## What that doesn't mean

It doesn't mean nobody is actually succeeding, and it doesn't mean your rejection wasn't real or doesn't matter. It means the specific feeling that "everyone else has this figured out except me," generated by scrolling a feed engineered in part around engagement mechanics this project has documented, is not a reliable read of reality. The sample you're seeing is distorted by design, not by your own shortcomings.

## The actual, practical takeaway

If a specific viral post's advice made you feel worse rather than more equipped, that reaction is worth trusting over the post's engagement count, a number this project's own research suggests carries far less signal about genuine value than it appears to.
