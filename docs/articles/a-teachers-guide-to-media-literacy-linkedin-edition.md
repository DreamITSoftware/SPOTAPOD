# A Teacher's Guide to Media Literacy, LinkedIn Edition

### A real, usable classroom example: a specific number that looked authoritative, and turned out to be wrong by a factor of three

Media literacy curricula usually reach for news examples, a misleading headline, a doctored image. LinkedIn engagement data offers a specific, concrete, classroom-usable example of a different and increasingly common failure mode: a plausible-sounding statistic that nobody rechecked before repeating it.

## The example, laid out simply

A document claimed 1,706 identical resume-rewrite posts existed in a LinkedIn dataset. Independently recomputing the same count directly from the raw data, a task any student could follow the logic of, even without running the code themselves, produced 533. Not a rounding difference. A number more than three times too high, apparently carried forward from an earlier, unverified citation.

## Why this example works well for teaching

It has no political charge, no emotional weight, and no ambiguity about which number is correct. The recomputation is a straightforward count, checkable in principle by anyone with access to the same data. That makes it a clean vehicle for the actual lesson: any specific number in a piece of content should be treated as a claim to check, not a fact to accept, regardless of how confidently or how many times it's been repeated.

## A second example worth pairing with it

This project also caught and corrected one of its own published numbers, a claim that nine of the ten highest-liked posts in a dataset showed zero views, which on recheck turned out to be four. Pairing the two examples, one from an outside source, one self-corrected, makes the lesson land without turning it into "don't trust other people," which is the wrong lesson. The right lesson is "recheck the specific number, regardless of source, including your own."

## A discussion prompt worth using

Ask students: if a claim gets repeated by several sources, does that make it more likely to be true? The 1,706 figure's apparent propagation (repeated rather than recomputed) is a useful counter-example. Repetition without independent verification adds the appearance of confirmation without any of its substance.

## What this is, and isn't

**VERIFIED**: 533 (not 1,706) identical resume-rewrite posts, and 4 of 10 (not 9 of 10) top-liked posts with zero views. Both are independently reproducible from source data.
