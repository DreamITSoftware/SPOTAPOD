# A Teacher's Guide to Media Literacy, LinkedIn Edition

### A classroom example: a specific number that looked authoritative and was wrong by a factor of three

Media literacy curricula usually reach for news examples, a misleading headline, a doctored image. LinkedIn engagement data offers a classroom example of a different failure: a plausible-sounding statistic that nobody rechecked before repeating it.

## The example

A document claimed 1,706 identical resume-rewrite posts existed in a LinkedIn dataset. Independently recomputing the same count directly from the raw data, a task any student could follow the logic of, even without running the code themselves, produced 533. That is not a rounding difference. The number was more than three times too high, apparently carried forward from an earlier, unverified citation.

## Why this example works for teaching

It has no political charge and no ambiguity about which number is correct. The recomputation is a straightforward count that anyone with the same data can check. The lesson is that any specific number in a piece of content is a claim to check, however confidently or often it has been repeated.

## A second example

This project also caught and corrected one of its own published numbers, a claim that nine of the ten highest-liked posts in a dataset showed zero views, which on recheck turned out to be four. Pairing the two examples, one from an outside source and one self-corrected, shows that the lesson is not "don't trust other people." It is "recheck the specific number, regardless of source, including your own."

## A discussion prompt

Ask students: if a claim gets repeated by several sources, does that make it more likely to be true? The 1,706 figure appears to have been repeated rather than recomputed, which makes it a counter-example. Repetition without independent verification is not confirmation.

## What this is, and isn't

**VERIFIED**: 533 (not 1,706) identical resume-rewrite posts, and 4 of 10 (not 9 of 10) top-liked posts with zero views. Both are independently reproducible from source data.
